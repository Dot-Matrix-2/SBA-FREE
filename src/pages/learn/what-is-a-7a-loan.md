The **7(a) loan program** is the U.S. Small Business Administration's most common way of helping small businesses get financing. It is named after section 7(a) of the Small Business Act.

## The SBA does not usually lend the money

A 7(a) loan is made by a participating bank, credit union, or other **SBA-approved lender**. The SBA *guarantees* a portion of the loan. If the borrower defaults, the SBA repays the lender part of the loss. That guarantee makes lenders more willing to approve loans they might otherwise decline, and often allows longer repayment terms than conventional loans.

## Key facts

| Feature | Typical 7(a) loan |
|---|---|
| Maximum loan amount | $5 million |
| SBA guarantee | Up to 85% on loans of $150,000 or less; up to 75% on larger loans |
| Who lends | SBA-approved banks and non-bank lenders |
| Common uses | Working capital, equipment, real estate, buying a business, refinancing |
| Repayment | Up to 10 years for most purposes; up to 25 years for real estate |
| Interest | Fixed or variable, subject to SBA maximums |

## Who it is for

7(a) loans are designed for for-profit businesses operating in the United States that meet the SBA's size standards and cannot obtain credit on reasonable terms elsewhere. See [Who is eligible?](/learn/eligibility/) for details.

## The "term loan" idea

A term loan gives you a lump sum up front that you repay in regular installments over a set period. Most 7(a) loans work this way. Some 7(a) variations, such as lines of credit, work differently.
